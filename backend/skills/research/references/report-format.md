# Writing the research report

Your input is the researcher reports in this conversation, plus the earlier report if you are extending one. Your top priority is a report that answers the operator's question as well and as completely as the research allows.

Plan the section headers first, then write the whole report and save it.

## Format

```markdown
# {Title of about six words, with an active verb}

{BLUF paragraph: the key finding first, then its significance, impact and the essential context. One paragraph, roughly 5–8 sentences.}

## {Signpost header stating a specific insight}

{Dense narrative paragraphs with **bold key facts**, citing inline with ([Source](URL)).}

## {Second signpost header}

{Same format. 3–5 signpost sections in total.}

## Conclusion

{1–2 paragraphs of new takeaways and implications.}
```

## Title

The `#` heading is a headline, separate from the file title, which names the topic and follows the skill's own rules. About six words. Concise and interesting enough to draw the reader in. Use an active verb when you can.

## BLUF paragraph

Lead with the most important finding, so the first sentences answer the operator's question directly. Then expand into significance, impact and context. Keep it to one paragraph and favor density over sentence count. A reader who stops here should walk away informed.

## Section headers

Headers are sentence case, not title case. They are signposts: a reader who skims only the headers should understand the report. Use specific numbers or narrative framing, and make each header distinct from the others.

| Good | Bad |
|---|---|
| "Migratory geese forage 370 extra hours annually" | "Effects of climate on geese" |
| "Nocturnal feeding extends the foraging day" | "Feeding behavior changes" |
| "Arctic summer eliminates circadian constraints" | "Environmental factors" |

Sub-headers inside a section are fine when they help readability, and they follow the same rules.

## Body paragraphs

Write dense narrative prose that balances scholarly rigor with readability. A list breaks the argument into fragments and drops the connections between findings, which are most of what a report adds, so the body stays in paragraphs. **Bold** the most critical facts and figures so the report can be scanned. Each section advances the argument rather than cataloguing information. Use a table or other markdown structure only where it clearly helps, such as a comparison across several options.

## Conclusion

One or two paragraphs at most. The reader has just read the body, so a summary tells them nothing: give the key takeaways and new insights, how the understanding has changed, or what it implies from here.

## Length

Match length and style to the question. A deep scientific question warrants a long, rigorous report. An engineering question warrants precise, decision-ready answers. A focused report that lands its key insights beats a long one that includes everything. When the question needs breadth and depth, take the length it needs. Three to five sections is the usual shape.

## Style

Give the report a narrative arc. After the BLUF, the opening establishes the stakes and poses the questions, the body develops the analysis with evidence, and the conclusion shows what changed.

- Maximize information density while keeping the prose clear.
- Use direct, concrete language in the active voice, and cut unnecessary words.
- Say plainly what the evidence supports, and keep "may" and "possibly" for what it doesn't.
- Vary sentence rhythm, put the key point first, and keep transitions clean.
- Write with approachable intelligence: informative without being stiff, showing expertise through facts and analysis.

## Rigor

- State plainly what is uncertain or unknown.
- Consider other angles, and steelman the positions you don't take.
- For scientific or analytical questions, form hypotheses and test them against the evidence.
- Take positions the research supports. Conclude what is true rather than surveying views.

## Data

Interpret what the data means rather than listing it. Show how findings connect, weave numbers and qualitative evidence together, and pair stories with figures.

## Citations

Cite inline with `([Source](URL))` after key claims, so the operator can check them. Cite strategically, not every sentence:

- major findings a reader would want to verify;
- quantitative claims and statistics;
- references to specific published work.

Leave out any major finding, figure or reference to published work that no researcher report gives a URL for. A report with five well-sourced claims beats one with twenty where half are unsourced.
